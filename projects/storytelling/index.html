AI-Enhanced Interactive Storytelling

Supporting executive functions and cultural learning through participatory design

Question
How can an AI-supported interactive story, grounded in a family’s culture, support children’s executive functions through parent–child play?
Context
M.Sc. thesis in Cognitive Science, Umeå University, 2025
Supervisor
Dr. Arzu Güneysu, Department of Computer Science
Methods
Participatory design, semi-structured interviews, reflexive thematic analysis, usability testing, high-fidelity prototyping
Output
Peer-reviewed poster, HAI ’25 (ACM) · doi.org/10.1145/3765766.3765806 (opens in a new tab)

What it is

An interactive digital narrative for children to play together with a parent. The child picks a cultural world (for example a Swedish Midsommar meadow) and fills it with characters, objects, animals and weather. Then the child co-creates a story with an AI storyteller.

The story is told in turns. The AI opens with a small mystery, and the child continues it. When the child gets stuck, a help button gives a hint instead of an answer. The story ends with tasks that ask the child to bring in a memory or a story from their own family, so the play becomes a conversation between parent and child.

Why

The goal was to support children's executive functions and cultural learning through parent–child interaction. Turn-taking, deciding what happens next, asking for help and returning to the task all ask the child to plan and keep the story in mind. The cultural worlds and the family-memory tasks connect the story to the child's own background.

How it was made

The prototype was co-designed through participatory design. It was shaped with semi-structured interviews and analysed with reflexive thematic analysis. It was then built as a high-fidelity prototype in Figma and evaluated through usability testing. The work was published as a peer-reviewed poster at the 13th International Conference on Human-Agent Interaction (HAI ’25).

What we found

Culture lives in stories, festivals and nature. In the first workshop, participants named three anchors of Swedish culture: Astrid Lindgren’s books, trolls and runestones; Midsommar and summer landscapes; and forests as spiritual places, with animals such as bears as morally complex figures. These became the story’s settings, heroes and antagonists.

Play that exercises executive functions. Participants proposed things for parent and child to do together: plan story choices, role-play characters who follow rules, and react to twists in the middle of the story. These ideas shaped the scenarios and the interaction flow.

Two roles for the AI. Wise-AI is a reflective guide that asks questions such as “What might happen if the troll takes the stone?” to prompt planning and perspective-taking. Aurora brings in culturally grounded twists. Both were designed as Figma interaction flows, not built as working AI.

Testing with seven participants. Most found the prototype enjoyable and culturally authentic: “It feels like a real Swedish story, but more playful.” Wise-AI was valued for prompting reflection, but its role needed to be clearer. Aurora was inspiring but sometimes interrupted the story, which suggests that families should be able to adjust when the AI steps in.

Looking back

The main lesson is about timing: when and how an AI steps into a shared story matters as much as what it says. Guidance has to be balanced against the risk of breaking the flow of play.

The evaluation involved a small group of adults, not children and parents at home, and the AI was scripted rather than implemented. So the study cannot show that the story improves executive functions or keeps families engaged over time. The next steps are to test with families, refine how Wise-AI and Aurora support the child, and look at long-term cognitive and cultural outcomes.
